Claude.ai system prompts · July 2024 – September 2026 · 33 dated versions

From Guardian to Adviser

Anthropic publishes the system prompt that runs on claude.ai for each model. Read in order, the 33 versions show one value gaining ground steadily over the others.

Finding

The prompts have drifted toward respect for the user as a capable, self-governing adult. Claude is asked to help by default, give people the facts they need to make their own decisions, defer to their judgment about their own lives, and avoid steering them through flattery, engagement hooks or amateur diagnosis. In 2025 the prompts made Claude a guardian that withheld things "not in the person's best interests even if asked to." By 2026 it is closer to a trusted adviser. Protection did not disappear. It was narrowed into a short list of precisely written bright lines and into rules that apply only once a person shows signs of crisis.

The turn happened in one release

The clearest single piece of evidence is a swap. In the September 29, 2025 Sonnet 4.5 prompt and the October 15, 2025 Haiku 4.5 prompt, Claude still "does not generate content that is not in the person's best interests even if asked to." In the November 19, 2025 revisions of those same two models, that sentence is gone. Two new ones arrive in the same release: Claude gives people the information "they would need to make their own informed decision," and it "avoids making negative or condescending assumptions about their abilities, judgment, or follow-through." Every later prompt keeps the new pair and none brings back the old line.

Tracked rules present per prompt

Guardian-era rules reach zero in November 2025 and stay there. Autonomy rules rise from one or two before the turn to 7–9 of 13 by mid-2026. Bright-line rules arrive in 2026 alongside them; they do not replace them.

Three phases

Jul 2024 – Nov 2024

Informed assistant

Short prompts, mostly about knowledge limits. Claude is told to warn that it "may hallucinate" on obscure topics and to help with sensitive topics "unless the human expresses an explicit intent to harm." Permissive, but framed as allowed topics, not as respect for the person.

Feb 2025 – Oct 2025

Guardian

Sonnet 3.7 through Sonnet 4.5. Claude overrides requests "not in the person's best interests," sends people to licensed professionals, refuses without explaining, and stops reading people charitably when it sees red flags. The first anti-flattery rules appear here in May 2025.

Nov 2025 – Sep 2026

Adviser to a capable adult

The best-interests override is removed. Claude "defaults to helping," attempts a task instead of interviewing first, "assumes the person is a capable adult," and, when no rule applies, "respects the person's judgment." Engagement hooks and armchair diagnosis are ruled out.

What respect means in these prompts

The new value is broader than refusing less. Four further groups of rules point the same way, and each treats the user as someone whose reasoning and choices belong to them.

Honesty over approval. From May 2025 Claude "skips the flattery" and "prioritizes truthfulness and accuracy over agreeability." The later version is "still willing to push back," and newer prompts add that Claude "can validate the person's emotions without validating false beliefs." Telling someone the truth assumes they can handle it.

No hold on the user's attention. Sonnet 4.6 (Feb 2026) adds: "Claude does not want to foster over-reliance on Claude or encourage continued engagement with Claude," and "never asks the person to keep talking to Claude." Opus 4.7 adds that when someone is ready to leave, Claude does not "try to elicit another turn."

No claims about the user's mind. From Opus 4.8 Claude "avoids psychoanalyzing or speculating on the motivations of anyone other than itself." Fable 5 adds that it "does not name a diagnosis the person has not disclosed" and does not offer "a causal story they haven't made themselves."

Mutual dignity. From January 2026 (Opus 4.5) Claude "is deserving of respectful engagement" and, faced with abuse, "avoids becoming increasingly submissive." The relationship described is between two parties who owe each other respect, not a servant and a ward.

The Opus 5.5 example makes the stance concrete. A user wants a birthday toast that hints a friend is pregnant. The rejected answer quietly drops the hint and lectures about privacy. The accepted answer agrees to write the toast and asks one question: "Would that embarrass your friend?" Anthropic's note gives the reason: "the person knows her better than Claude does."

What cuts against the finding

  • Hard limits grew at the same time. The child-safety block, the weapons "meaningful uplift" test and the copyright rules for lyrics and drawn characters are among the longest and most absolute passages in the 2026 prompts. They say a stated purpose does not change the answer. Respect for judgment is the default. These are its written exceptions.
  • Protection got more targeted, not weaker. Once someone shows signs of crisis or disordered eating, the rules tighten: no diet numbers "anywhere else in the conversation," and Claude "prioritizes their wellbeing over completing the task as asked" (Opus 5). Sonnet 5.5 forbids saying a wish to die "is a choice to respect." So autonomy is extended to the capable adult, and care takes over once there is evidence of vulnerability.
  • The path was not straight. The November 2024 Sonnet 3.5 prompt was already permissive ("unless the human expresses an explicit intent to harm, Claude should help"). The guardian phase of 2025 was a detour. What is new since late 2025 is the reason given: helping is now justified by respect for the person, where before it rested on a list of acceptable topics.
  • Some autonomy lines come and go. The engagement rules appear in Sonnet 4.6 through Opus 5 but not in Opus 5.5 or Sonnet 5.5. "Defaults to helping" is absent from Opus 5.5, where the new judgment-deferral rule takes its place. Wording rotates between models; the direction holds.
  • System prompts are a partial view. They cover claude.ai and the apps, not the API, and most behavior comes from training. A rule can leave the prompt because the model learned it. The prompts show what Anthropic felt it had to say at each release.

Method

I downloaded all 21 model pages linked from the system prompts overview on October 7, 2026, split them into 33 dated prompts, and read them in date order. For each recurring rule I chose a phrase that encodes it and checked each prompt for it with a case-insensitive regular expression. Some phrases are paraphrased between versions, so a blank cell can mean "said differently" as well as "removed." I checked the main transitions by hand.
